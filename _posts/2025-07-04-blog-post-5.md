In recent years, large language models (LLMs) like GPT-4, Claude, LLaMA, and DeepSeek have dominated the AI landscape. It feels like every tech company is racing to build the next biggest, smartest model—each one trying to outdo the last in size, performance, and capabilities.

But here’s a question we don’t ask often enough:  
**Do you know how much data it takes to pretrain a large language model?**

💡 *Fun fact*: GPT-3 was trained on such a massive amount of text that if a human tried to read it all, 24/7 without sleep, it would take them over 2,600 years!

And that’s just the data. What about the memory and compute cost?  
Training these behemoths can cost hundreds of millions (even billions) of dollars, not to mention the massive energy consumption. It’s clear that while LLMs are powerful, they’re not always practical—especially if you want cost-efficiency, sustainability, and real-time performance.

Now compare that to **Vicuna‑13B**, a lightweight open‑source chatbot developed by LMSYS and universities like Berkeley and Stanford. It was fine‑tuned from LLaMA using around 70,000 user-shared ChatGPT conversations—and astonishingly, the entire process cost only about $300 in cloud compute. According to a GPT‑4-based evaluation, Vicuna achieves over 90% of ChatGPT/Bard’s response quality, outperforming Alpaca and LLaMA in more than 90% of cases.

This shows that we don’t always need to train or deploy massive LLMs to build capable, high-performing systems. With careful fine-tuning and efficient design, **small language models (SLMs)** can deliver impressive results—often at a fraction of the cost and computational burden. While the race to build ever-larger models continues, it’s equally important that we start giving attention to small language models.

## The Power of Small Language Models

Small language models may not grab headlines like their massive counterparts, but they’re quietly revolutionizing how we build practical, efficient AI systems. Let’s look at a few small-yet-mighty models and what they bring to the table:

### 💬 Mistral 7B – Lightning-Fast Reasoning
Mistral is a decoder-only model that competes with larger LLMs on reasoning, coding, and general conversation, but at lower latency and memory cost. It supports **int4 quantization**, making it perfect for edge deployment.  
**Why it matters**: Ideal for local inference, chatbots, and even mobile AI apps.

### 🐦 Phi-2 (2.7B) – Tiny Yet Surprisingly Smart
Trained by Microsoft Research, Phi-2 is only 2.7B in size but excels in **common-sense reasoning**, **math**, and **code generation**—often matching or beating much larger models.  
**Why it matters**: Great for education tools, tutors, or copilots where size and interpretability matter.

### 🤖 TinyLLaMA (1.1B) – Language Understanding in 1GB
Designed for extremely constrained environments, TinyLLaMA shows that useful natural language understanding can still happen in ~1GB.  
**Why it matters**: Enables language-based applications on low-resource devices like Raspberry Pi or mobile.

### 💡 Gemma 2B – Responsible and Efficient
Google’s Gemma 2B is optimized for **open and responsible AI use**. It’s instruction-tuned and supports a wide range of tasks like summarization, question answering, and reasoning.  
**Why it matters**: You can build applications with strong performance and alignment without needing GPU farms.

What makes this model particularly exciting is that it supports both **text and vision**, meaning it can process and generate responses that combine written language and visual inputs—just like some of the largest vision-language models, but at a fraction of the size and cost.

## Features of Qwen2.5-VL-3B

- **Multimodal support**: Accepts both images and text as input  
- **Open-source**: Fully available on platforms like Hugging Face and GitHub  
- **Lightweight**: At just 3B parameters, it’s feasible to fine-tune on personal or lab-grade hardware  
- **RoPE scaling & better training stability**: Makes it suitable for long-context tasks and downstream customization

## Qwen2.5‑3B Architecture Explained

Here’s what makes this compact multimodal model tick:

## 1. Core Transformer Backbone

- **Decoder-only transformer**: Think of it as a big "brain" that reads and writes text. It has 36 layers and ~3.1B parameters (~2.8B in non-embedding weights).
- **Grouped Query Attention (GQA)**: Normally, transformers look at all words at once which is very slow. Qwen2.5 uses “Grouped Query Attention,” which splits this into efficient groups to speed things up without losing accuracy. It shares key/value processing across heads instead of giving every layer full attention resources, making it faster and cheaper to run. 
- **Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)**: It supports long-context comprehension—up to 32K tokens—allowing the model to handle lengthy documents or conversations.
- **Training optimizations**: It employs **SwiGLU activations**, **RMSNorm**, attention biases, and tied word embeddings for smoother and more stable training.

## 2. Vision-Language Integration

- **Image Encoder**: Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B inherits a ViT-based image encoder that transforms images and video frames into tokens that the text transformer can understand.
- **Smart Attention (Windowed + Global)**:  It uses **windowed attention** for efficiency with global attention only at select layers. Instead of analyzing an entire high-resolution image at once (which would be very slow), the model looks at small windows most of the time, but switches to full view occasionally—combining speed with accuracy.
- **Dynamic resolution & FPS scaling**: As images comes in all sizes, it adjusts to varying image/video sizes and frame rates. It uses **Multimodal RoPE (mRoPE)** to encode spatial and temporal embeddings.
- **Advanced capabilities**: It's able to locate objects, highlight tables, or point to specific spots—useful for tasks like document scanning or diagram interpretation. It supports advanced capabilities like **bounding-box detection**, **document parsing**, **chart/diagram understanding**, and **visual-agent reasoning**.

## 3. Instruction Tuning & Fine‑Tuning

- **Massive Pretraining (18 trillion tokens)**
  The model absorbed a huge amount of text—far more than earlier versions—to build strong language abilities. 
- **Instruction Fine-Tuning (>1 million examples)**
  It was then taught how to respond to instructions, like “Describe this image” or “Answer this question,” learning real interaction patterns.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RLHF: DPO & GRPO)**
  To sound more helpful and human-friendly, it used advanced alignment techniques called Direct Preference Optimization and GRPO.
- **Multimodal Training**:
On the multimodal side, it learns on multimodal datasets (image‑text pairs), teaching the image encoder and text decoder to work together. The training includes image-text, video-text, and audio-text, aligning the vision encoder to the LLM backbone.
- **Fusion**: Once the image is processed, the output tokens merge into the language stream, allowing seamless joint reasoning across both modalities.

## 4. Modular Architecture & Extendability

- **Separate modal encoders**: Each modality (text, image, video/audio) has its own encoder but all are integrated into a shared transformer via token embeddings.

Although the 3B variant doesn’t support audio or video, its sibling, **Qwen2.5-Omni-7B** architecture reveals it's full multimodal potential. It shows how the architecture can scale:

- **Thinker–Talker split**: "Thinker" generates meaning; "Talker" handles speech generation. This separation prevents interference between modalities.
- **TMRoPE (Time-aligned Multimodal RoPE)**: Aligns audio and video frames across time, crucial for lip-sync and synchronized outputs.
- **Streaming mechanisms**: Combines block-wise encoding and sliding-window decoding to support real-time, low-latency interactions.

## 🔧 Fine-Tuning Qwen2.5‑VL‑3B with LoRA on LLaVAR-2

After understanding Qwen2.5-VL-3B’s architecture, let’s dive into how we actually **fine-tune** the model on a specialized multimodal dataset like **LLaVAR-2**. 

LLaVAR-2 is a **text-rich vision-language dataset** curated to improve the model’s ability to understand complex visual data: charts, diagrams, forms, documents, and screenshots. Fine-tuning on such data enables Qwen2.5-VL to perform tasks like document QA, UI analysis, and technical visual comprehension. The LLaVAR-2 is a dataset with benchmarks like OCRBench, ST-VQA, TexTVQA, DocVQA and many more. 

To adapt the model without retraining all 3B parameters, we use **parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)** with **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)**.

---

### 📦 LoRA Setup

In our training pipeline:

* We insert LoRA adapters into selected transformer and vision layers like `q_proj`, `v_proj`, `gate_proj`, `fc1`, and `fc2`.
* We use **rank 64**, **alpha 64**, and **dropout 0.05** to balance adaptability and regularization.
* Only these adapters are trainable — the rest of the model stays frozen.

This setup significantly reduces GPU memory usage and speeds up training. Thanks to this, we can even train the model using **8-bit quantization** and **gradient checkpointing**, further optimizing memory without compromising performance. **Cosime learning rate scheduler** was added for smoother convergence.

---

### 🎯 Training Objective: Cross-Entropy Loss for Vision-Language Modeling

We train using **causal language modeling (CLM)** with **cross-entropy loss**, which aligns naturally with autoregressive generation:

* Inputs include both **image embeddings** (from the Vision Transformer) and **text tokens**.
* The model predicts the **next token** in the sequence, just like in text-only LLMs.
* Special multimodal tokens (e.g., `<image>`, `<bbox>`, `<ocr>`) guide the model to align visual input with text output.

The **cross-entropy loss** penalizes wrong predictions and helps the model better connect what it sees (images) with what it says (text).

---

### 🧠 Custom Training Logic: `QwenVLCustomTrainer`

We implement a **custom Hugging Face `Trainer` subclass** to handle the quirks of vision-language modeling. Here's how it helps:

#### 🧩 `compute_loss()`

* Shifts logits and labels to match CLM requirements.
* Masks out `IGNORE_INDEX` tokens — so padding, OCR spans, or non-target tokens don’t affect the loss.
* Handles **edge cases** like empty labels or faulty batches gracefully.
* Applies **loss scaling** when the model becomes unstable (e.g., early-stage spikes).
* Tracks loss history to help visualize convergence.

#### 🌀 `training_step()`

* Runs forward + backward pass, and manages gradient accumulation.
* Clips gradients (`max_norm = 0.5`) to avoid exploding gradients.
* Monitors **gradient norms** every 50 steps — useful for catching overfitting or vanishing gradients.
* Detects and skips training steps with **NaN or Inf loss** — critical for robust multimodal training.


```bash

class QwenVLCustomTrainer(Trainer):
    """Custom trainer for Qwen2.5-VL with improved loss computation"""
    
    def __init__(self, *args, **kwargs):
        super().__init__(*args, **kwargs)
        self.loss_history = []
        self.gradient_norms = []
        self.best_loss = float('inf')
        self.best_checkpoint_path = None
        
    def compute_loss(self, model, inputs, return_outputs=False, 
                     num_items_in_batch=None):
        """
        Custom loss computation with better handling for vision-language models
        """
        labels = inputs.get("labels")
        if labels is None:
            raise ValueError("Labels are required for loss computation")
            
        # Forward pass
        outputs = model(**inputs)
        logits = outputs.logits
        
        # Get valid (non-ignored) labels for loss computation
        valid_labels = labels != IGNORE_INDEX
        num_valid_tokens = valid_labels.sum().item()
        
        if num_valid_tokens == 0:
            # If no valid tokens, return zero loss
            logger.warning("No valid tokens found for loss computation")
            loss = torch.tensor(0.0, device=logits.device, requires_grad=True)
        else:
            # Shift logits and labels for causal LM
            shift_logits = logits[..., :-1, :].contiguous()
            shift_labels = labels[..., 1:].contiguous()
            
            # Flatten for loss computation
            flat_logits = shift_logits.view(-1, shift_logits.size(-1))
            flat_labels = shift_labels.view(-1)
            
            # Compute cross entropy loss with ignore index
            loss_fct = nn.CrossEntropyLoss(
                ignore_index=IGNORE_INDEX,
                reduction='mean'
            )
            loss = loss_fct(flat_logits, flat_labels)
            
            # Gradient scaling for stability
            if loss.item() > 10.0:  # If loss is too high, scale it down
                loss = loss * 0.1
                logger.warning(f"High loss detected ({loss.item():.4f}), "
                               "applying gradient scaling")
        
        # Store loss for tracking (reduced frequency)
        self.loss_history.append(loss.item())
        if len(self.loss_history) > 100:
            self.loss_history = self.loss_history[-100:]
        
        return (loss, outputs) if return_outputs else loss
    
    def training_step(self, model, inputs, num_items_in_batch=None):
        """
        Custom training step with gradient monitoring
        """
        model.train()
        
        # Forward pass and loss computation
        loss = self.compute_loss(model, inputs, 
                                 num_items_in_batch=num_items_in_batch)
        
        # Handle case where compute_loss returns tuple (loss, outputs)
        if isinstance(loss, tuple):
            loss = loss[0]
        
        # Backward pass with gradient scaling
        if self.args.gradient_accumulation_steps > 1:
            loss = loss / self.args.gradient_accumulation_steps
            
        # Check for NaN or infinite loss
        if torch.isnan(loss) or torch.isinf(loss):
            logger.warning(f"Invalid loss detected: {loss.item()}, "
                           "skipping step")
            return torch.tensor(0.0, device=loss.device, requires_grad=True)
        
        self.accelerator.backward(loss)
        
        # Apply gradient clipping
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=0.5)
        
        # Monitor gradient norms (reduced frequency)
        if self.state.global_step % 50 == 0:  # Every 50 steps instead of 10
            total_norm = 0.0
            param_count = 0
            
            for name, param in model.named_parameters():
                if param.grad is not None and param.requires_grad:
                    param_norm = param.grad.data.norm(2)
                    total_norm += param_norm.item() ** 2
                    param_count += 1
            
            if param_count > 0:
                total_norm = total_norm ** (1. / 2)
                self.gradient_norms.append(total_norm)
                
                # Keep only last 100 gradient norms
                if len(self.gradient_norms) > 100:
                    self.gradient_norms = self.gradient_norms[-100:]
                
                # Only warn if gradient norm is extremely high
                if total_norm > 5.0:
                    logger.warning(f"High gradient norm: {total_norm:.4f}")
        
        return loss.detach()
    
    def save_model(self, output_dir=None, _internal_call=False):
        """Override save_model to track best checkpoint"""
        if output_dir is None:
            output_dir = self.args.output_dir
            
        # Call parent save_model
        super().save_model(output_dir, _internal_call)
        
        # Check if this is the best checkpoint based on recent loss
        if self.loss_history:
            current_avg_loss = (sum(self.loss_history[-10:]) / 
                                len(self.loss_history[-10:]))
            if current_avg_loss < self.best_loss:
                self.best_loss = current_avg_loss
                self.best_checkpoint_path = output_dir
                logger.info(f"New best checkpoint: {output_dir} "
                            f"(loss: {current_avg_loss:.4f})")
                
                # Save best checkpoint path to file
                best_path_file = (f"{self.args.output_dir}/"
                                  "best_checkpoint_path.txt")
                with open(best_path_file, 'w') as f:
                    f.write(f"Best checkpoint path: "
                            f"{self.best_checkpoint_path}\n")
                    f.write(f"Best loss: {self.best_loss:.6f}\n")
                    f.write(f"Step: {self.state.global_step}\n")
    
    def on_save(self, args, state, control, **kwargs):
        """Called when saving checkpoint"""
        checkpoint_dir = f"{args.output_dir}/checkpoint-{state.global_step}"
        
        # Check if this is the best checkpoint
        if self.loss_history:
            current_avg_loss = (sum(self.loss_history[-10:]) / 
                                len(self.loss_history[-10:]))
            if current_avg_loss < self.best_loss:
                self.best_loss = current_avg_loss
                self.best_checkpoint_path = checkpoint_dir
                logger.info(f"New best checkpoint: {checkpoint_dir} "
                            f"(loss: {current_avg_loss:.4f})")
                
                # Save best checkpoint path to file
                best_path_file = f"{args.output_dir}/best_checkpoint_path.txt"
                with open(best_path_file, 'w') as f:
                    f.write(f"Best checkpoint path: "
                            f"{self.best_checkpoint_path}\n")
                    f.write(f"Best loss: {self.best_loss:.6f}\n")
                    f.write(f"Step: {state.global_step}\n")
    
    def log(self, logs: Dict[str, float], start_time=None):
        """
        Enhanced logging with reduced verbosity
        """
        # Add custom metrics
        if self.loss_history:
            logs["best_loss"] = self.best_loss
            
        # Call parent log method
        super().log(logs, start_time)
        
        # Reduced frequency detailed logging - only every 100 steps
        if self.state.global_step % 100 == 0:
            logger.info(f"Step {self.state.global_step}: "
                        f"Loss = {logs.get('loss', 'N/A'):.4f}, "
                        f"Best = {self.best_loss:.4f}")
            if self.best_checkpoint_path:
                logger.info(f"Best checkpoint: {self.best_checkpoint_path}")

```

### 🔍 Targeting the Right Layers for LoRA

We use the helper function `find_target_linear_names()` to dynamically locate linear layers for LoRA insertion:

```python
target_modules = [

```

These layers are most responsible for:

* Modality alignment between vision and language
* Attention fusion between tokens and image regions
* Feature transformation and compression inside the ViT

By only training adapters in these layers, we let the model adapt to **text-heavy images** like documents and charts — without losing its general capabilities.

### 📉 Loss Monitoring & Stability Techniques

Multimodal training is tricky — bad inputs or misaligned tokens can cause loss explosions or collapse. So we implement several safeguards:

| Mechanism                   | Purpose                                               |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Cross-entropy loss**      | Core loss for token prediction                        |
| **IGNORE\_INDEX**           | Avoids loss on padding, masked tokens, or image slots |
| **Loss scaling**            | Reduces instability when loss spikes                  |
| **Gradient clipping**       | Keeps training stable under noisy gradients           |
| **Gradient norm tracking**  | Provides diagnostics every 50 steps                   |
| **Skip NaN/Inf loss steps** | Ensures training won’t crash on bad batches           |

These tools let us train reliably — even with noisy OCR spans, overlapping bounding boxes, or variable-length image tokens.

## 📈 Benchmarking Qwen2.5‑VL on TextVQA & ChartQA

To evaluate how well our fine-tuned model understands and reasons over text-rich images, we benchmarked it using two widely-used datasets from the LLaVAR-2 suite: TextVQA, ChartQA and DocVQA. These benchmarks allow us to assess a model's ability to perform OCR-based reasoning, comprehension, and visual-text alignment in real-world scenarios.


### 🔍 Why These Benchmarks Matter

* **TextVQA** requires reading and reasoning over text embedded in images. It's representative of real-world scenarios like interpreting receipts, menus, or PDFs.
* **ChartQA** tests the ability to understand and reason over visualizations—bar charts, line graphs, tables, and more.
* **DocVQA** evaluates a model’s ability to understand and extract information from visually complex documents such as invoices, forms, and reports. It requires reasoning over both textual and layout elements within scanned document images.

Both tests demand high-quality OCR, precise visual-text alignment, and robust logical reasoning.
